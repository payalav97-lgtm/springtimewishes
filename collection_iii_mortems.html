mortem[s]

iii — mortem[s] began as a study on what remains after a body is no longer present.

The collection was not built from the idea of death as spectacle, but from the quieter and more disturbing space around it: the garment left behind, the object that absorbs what the body cannot keep, the trace that continues to exist after a person has been turned into an image.

I was interested in the way fabric can behave like memory. It wrinkles, stains, stretches, softens, loses shape, and still remains. A garment can look as if it has survived something before it ever reaches the body. It can feel inherited, recovered, unfinished, or almost forensic. For mortem[s], I wanted each piece to carry that tension: not clothing as fantasy, but clothing as evidence.

The research around the collection moved through Victorian images of drowned women, mourning garments, relics, hair, locks, water, silence, and the recurring cultural habit of turning female pain into something beautiful, still, and consumable. I kept returning to the question of what happens when the image of a woman is preserved more carefully than her voice.

mortem[s] is not an attempt to recreate that image. It is an attempt to disturb it.

The garments are built around interruption. Closures are exposed or displaced. Ties hang too long. Edges remain raw. Seams behave like repairs, wounds, or records. Some pieces refuse symmetry. Others sit close to the body but never fully resolve. The prototypes were photographed and processed as rescued documents because they are not final objects yet. They are studies, remnants, and incomplete proofs of a collection still forming.

This archive is part of the collection’s process, not separate from it. The scans, angel studies, treated photographs, garment fragments, and rough drawings all belong to the same language. They show the collection before it becomes clean, before it becomes final, before it is made into something easy to consume.

iii — mortem[s] lives in that unfinished space: between garment and relic, body and evidence, mourning and construction, disappearance and return.
